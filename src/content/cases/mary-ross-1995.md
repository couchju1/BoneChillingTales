In February 1995, Mary K. Ross gave Robert Power the key to her apartment. [S2]

She wanted a copy made for her babysitter. [S2]

Five months later, two men he hired used a key to walk into that apartment while she slept. [S2, S3]

The Supreme Court couldn't say how Power got the key they used. [S2]

## Westport

Mary was 25. She was studying to be a paralegal. She had a baby girl, Kristian, who was 14 months old that summer. [S4, S7]

On March 26, 1994, she signed a lease at the Westport Apartments in Sioux Falls for herself and her daughter. [S2, S8] Westport was a low-income development. [S2] Her apartment was on the third floor, the Argus Leader reported. [S8]

* * *

The apartment came with a sheet of policies. One of them read:

> **WESTPORT APARTMENT POLICIES**, as quoted by the South Dakota Supreme Court:
>
> "lock changes or additional locks are not permitted, it being understood that owner or manager may be required to enter in an emergency when no resident is present, such as fire or water coming through the ceiling." [S2]

A lost key cost $45. That covered a new lock if one was needed. A tenant who couldn't pay right away could have it taken out of the security deposit. [S2]

* * *

That is about as much as the sources found for this story hold about Mary's daily life.

Nothing in the sources found for this story says where she grew up, where she worked or what she liked to do.

"Mary K. was the fabric that held our family together," her brother, Shand Ross, told the parole board in 2022. [S6]

## Robert Power

Mary had a close friend named Amy Power. KELOLAND called her Mary's best friend. [S2, S4]

The Supreme Court spells her name Amy. KELOLAND spells it Aimee Enright Power. [S2, S4]

When Amy's marriage went bad, Mary let her move in. She gave her a key. [S2] The landlords say they were never told Amy was living there. [S8]

KELOLAND reported that Mary was helping Aimee escape an abusive marriage. [S4]

* * *

In May 1995, the trouble between Amy and her husband, Robert Power, got worse. [S2]

The courts recorded what Power believed. He believed Mary was interfering with his marriage. He was upset that she had smoked marijuana in front of his child. [S3]

"Power wanted Ross killed because she was interfering in his marriage," KELOLAND crime reporter Jessica Armstrong said in 1995. [S4]

* * *

Power went to Michael Lee Smith. [S1]

According to the Supreme Court, he offered Smith $10,000 or two pounds of methamphetamine to kill Mary. Smith said no. But he agreed to find someone who would. [S1]

Smith introduced Power to two of the others. He was to handle the payment and keep $1,500 to $2,000 for himself. [S1]

The two men were Smith's roommates, Eric Coon and Robert Poppen. [S4] Smith was 21. [S1] The five men in the case ranged in age from 18 to 22. [S4]

* * *

The prosecutor, Dave Nelson, later described the deal differently. A pound of methamphetamine and $10,000. [S4] The Supreme Court wrote that Coon and Poppen were each to get $10,000. [S3]

"And he didn't have either one," Nelson said. [S4]

* * *

Power gave Smith a key to Mary's apartment and a map of its floor plan. [S1]

At his trial, Smith said he owned a key-making machine and had used it to copy the key to Mary's apartment. That testimony comes from the trial transcript as cited in a 2003 South Dakota Law Review note. [S8]

He said it had to happen the weekend of July 9, when his wife would be out of the state. [S1]

## The missing key

Amy lost her key ring. Mary's apartment key was on it. [S2]

The Supreme Court put the loss in July 1995. The landlords' brief, as quoted in a 2003 law review note, put it in June, in the parking lot, as Amy was leaving the complex with Robert. [S2, S8]

Mary told the maintenance man, Watson Lewing. He told the manager, Jodi Bentz, that one of the keys to Mary's apartment was missing. [S2]

* * *

What happened next was later disputed under oath. [S2]

Lewing said in a deposition that he didn't remember whether Mary asked for a new lock. [S2]

Bentz said she asked Lewing whether Mary wanted the lock changed. He told her Mary had said no, because she thought the keys would "turn up." [S2]

The next day, Bentz said, she asked Mary herself. Mary said no. [S2]

A third employee, Monica Price, told it the same way. [S2]

* * *

Mary's mother, Sherry Smith, said she talked to her daughter often on the phone. She shares a last name with Michael Lee Smith. No source connects them. [S1, S2]

Her sworn account, as the court summed it up:

> **SHERRY SMITH**, affidavit, as summarized by the South Dakota Supreme Court:
>
> "(1) she maintained frequent phone contact with Ross"
>
> "(2) she spoke with Ross three days before the murder and Ross told her that she had requested a lock change"
>
> "(3) Ross told her she needed money to pay for a lock change"
>
> "(4) she detected fear in Ross's voice, and Ross refused to discuss the Robert and Amy Power situation, telling her mother, 'I do not want to talk about him. He's psycho.'" [S2]

* * *

The lock wasn't changed. [S2]

## The Hy-Vee

Power gave Coon a map of the apartment and the parking lot, a key, the keys to his car and $50. [S3, S4]

The $50 was for supplies. [S3]

At a Hy-Vee grocery store, Coon and Poppen bought two knives and two pairs of gloves. They also bought cigarettes, soda and a frying pan, to give to Poppen's girlfriend. [S3]

Poppen explained the frying pan later. "It would look kind of fishy if we just went in and bought knives and gloves." [S3]

* * *

They believed killing her would make people afraid of them. It would give their egos a boost, the court wrote. [S3]

Poppen said it was all about "power and money." [S3]

They thought Power would pay. He "seemed like a nice enough person," they said. [S3]

## 4 a.m.

Around 4 a.m. on Sunday, July 9, 1995, Coon and Poppen let themselves into Mary's apartment with a key. [S3, S4]

The lights were off. [S4] She was asleep in her bedroom. [S3] Her daughter was in the apartment, the prosecutor said later. [S4]

* * *

They stabbed her. [S3]

Their knives broke. They went through her kitchen drawers for more. [S3]

Mary got away to the kitchen and called 911. [S3, S4]

* * *

Jessica Armstrong read the 911 transcript on KELOLAND in 1995:

> **MARY K. ROSS**, to the 911 dispatcher, July 9, 1995, as read by Jessica Armstrong:
>
> "I need help. I've been stabbed."
>
> Asked where:
>
> "All over."
>
> The last words the dispatcher could make out:
>
> "I have a baby." [S4]

* * *

The men left her on the living room couch. They thought she was dead. [S3]

The court counted 16 stab and sharp-force wounds. [S3]

Police found her baby unharmed in her crib, the Argus Leader reported. [S8] At a later court hearing, Poppen said they hadn't known she had a child. [S8]

## The address book

Investigators were at the apartment within about an hour. [S3]

From Mary's address book and from informants, they put together a list of suspects. [S3]

* * *

On August 1, detectives began rounding people up. They hadn't planned to have everyone at the station at once, but that is how it went. They were put in separate rooms. Some rooms had tape running. Detectives went from room to room as the story came together. [S3]

Coon and Poppen each waived their rights. Each confessed. [S3]

* * *

Smith came in that afternoon with his girlfriend, who was being questioned too. He walked past one of the men who had done the stabbing. [S1]

His first interview ran from 1:15 to 2:40. He wasn't under arrest. On the tape he starts out confident. As he keeps talking, he gets nervous. At one point he cries. [S1]

He insisted Power never told him the name of the person he wanted dead. The detectives tried several times to get him to say otherwise. [S1]

By then, the people in the other rooms had named him. Before his second interview, at 4:50, police decided to hold him and read him his rights. He talked again until 5:17. [S1]

That evening, he said Power had hired Coon and Poppen. [S3]

## A major miracle

Coon's and Poppen's lawyers moved to throw out the confessions. [S3]

The case could carry the death penalty. "It was clearly a death case because it was murder for hire," Nelson said years later. [S4]

The defense asked for a deal with a term of years. The State's Attorney wasn't interested. It would be life or death, they were told. [S3]

* * *

One of Coon's lawyers wrote to the other:

> **LETTER BETWEEN COON'S DEFENSE LAWYERS**, as entered at his habeas hearing:
>
> "I have only reviewed the reports themselves, and did those rather hurriedly. From the basis of those reports, from talking to the other attorneys, and from talking to our client, a major miracle would be necessary to get an acquittal. There are a couple areas of law that I need to research, but I think I know the answer already. The area that we hopefully will get our best results are in the death penalty area. That will also be a struggle." [S3]

Poppen's lawyer said the case "had the death penalty written all over it." [S3]

* * *

Poppen pleaded guilty on December 15, 1995. He told the judge one reason was that he was afraid of the death penalty. [S3]

On December 18, Coon pleaded guilty. So did Robert Power. So did a fifth man, Walter Craft Jr. [S3]

Coon and Poppen were sentenced to life. The conspiracy charges against them were dropped, and they would not face death. As part of the deals, they agreed to testify against the others. [S3]

KELOLAND reported that Power pleaded guilty to first-degree murder and got life without parole. [S5] The Supreme Court says the same. [S2]

* * *

KELOLAND reported that Craft was charged with accessory to murder for throwing away Mary's clothing, and that he got less than three years. [S4] [VERIFY] The court records found for this story don't give his charge or his sentence. [S3]

## The videotape

Only Smith went to trial. [S3]

All four of the others testified against him. [S1]

On the first day of testimony, in March 1996, the jury heard Mary's 911 call. She told the operator, Sandy Kinder, that she didn't know who had attacked her, according to the Argus Leader. [S8]

His videotaped interviews were shown to the jury, with any mention of his past convictions and his time in prison cut out. [S1]

* * *

The tapes went back into the jury room with the other exhibits. There was no TV or VCR in there. [S1]

The jurors sent out a note. The tapes, they wrote, were doing them "little good sitting on the table." [S1]

The judge said no. They asked again. The judge brought them back into the courtroom and played the tapes a second time. [S1]

* * *

The jury convicted Smith of accessory to murder and conspiracy to commit murder. At a separate trial he was found to be a habitual offender. [S1]

He got ten years for the accessory count and life without parole for the conspiracy, to run together. [S1] KELOLAND later summed it up as life. [S5]

He appealed, saying his confession wasn't voluntary. On January 14, 1998, the South Dakota Supreme Court turned him down. [S1]

## A locked door

Sherry Smith sued Westport's owner and its management company, for Mary's estate and for Kristian. [S2]

A circuit judge threw the case out. Landlords, the judge ruled, owed no duty to protect tenants like Mary. [S2]

* * *

On March 13, 2002, the Supreme Court sent it back, three votes to two. [S2]

Justice Konenkamp wrote for the court. Somebody had to decide whether Mary asked for a new lock. Her mother said yes. The employees said no. At that stage, the court had to assume she had asked. [S2]

> **JUSTICE KONENKAMP**, Smith v. Lagow, 2002:
>
> "A home is a citadel, and its integrity depends, at least in part, on its locks. A locked door is the first defense to a violent world." [S2]

"If her lock had been changed beforehand, perhaps she would not have died," he wrote. "Only defendants' employees could have changed her lock." [S2]

* * *

The court also wrote that it wasn't clear how Power got a key. He might have copied the one Mary lent him in February. He might have had a chance in June, when he and his wife left Mary's apartment and Amy thought she had lost her keys in the parking lot. [S2]

* * *

Chief Justice Gilbertson dissented. Mary had given Power access to her key five months before her death, he wrote. She had given a key to Amy too. Her side never claimed the landlord knew about the trouble between the Powers, or even that Amy was living there. [S2]

The case went back to the trial court. [S2] As of January 2003, the family's lawyer said, no trial date had been set. [S8] The sources found for this story don't say how it ended.

## Second chances

In South Dakota, life means no parole. But a lifer can ask for clemency, which only the governor can grant, and can ask again every year. [S6, S7]

All four men asked. [S5] Poppen was turned down by a two-person panel five times. [S6] Coon was turned down for a hearing in 2019. [S7]

In 2022, Coon got a hearing in front of the full Board of Pardons and Paroles. [S6, S7]

* * *

Mary's family put out a statement:

> **THE FAMILY OF MARY K. ROSS**, to KELOLAND News, August 2022:
>
> "If we could bring back our amazing and much-loved Mary K., our world would be filled with the joy and laughter she brought to all our lives. We miss her more than anyone can imagine. She has been absent a lifetime with her precious daughter and never had a chance to know her grandchildren." [S4]

They organized a letter-writing campaign against any shorter sentence. [S4]

* * *

The hearing was on Wednesday, August 10, 2022, at the state penitentiary in Sioux Falls. Family members came from around the country. [S4, S5, S6]

Coon was 46. [S6]

> **ERIC COON**, before the Board of Pardons and Paroles, August 10, 2022:
>
> "I took a mom away from her child. I took a child away from her mom and her family. And there's nothing I can do to bring her back. I truly today apologize to the family. I wish I could turn around and face you." [S6]

The board's rules didn't let him turn around. [S6]

* * *

Terry Van Zanten ran the prison metal shop. "The Eric that started for us is not the Eric we know today," he said. [S6]

Minnehaha County State's Attorney Daniel Haggar spoke against it. He cited 90 violations in prison, from theft to assault to escape. [S6]

"They didn't want to be here," Haggar said of the family. [S6]

* * *

Mary's sister held up her photo. [S6]

"Some claim that his accomplishments show he is a changed man, but what might Mary K.'s accomplishments over the past 27 years have been?" she said. [S6]

Kristian was there. [S6, S7]

"He deserves to stay here the rest of his life," she said. "My entire life was changed and she didn't even get to know why. She didn't deserve it." [S6]

"If you're sorry, don't ever do this to my family again. Don't ask for commutation." [S6]

* * *

The board voted unanimously against recommending a shorter sentence. [S6]

Shand Ross talked to KELOLAND afterward. Did the apology mean anything? "Not really," he said. [S6]

"I hope this is a one-time deal, but if we do have to do it, I will be up here every single year." [S6]

## Life means life

On January 23, 2024, Mary's sister Rebecca Brindle testified before a South Dakota Senate committee. [S7]

The committee was hearing a bill that would make inmates serving life for violent crimes wait up to four years between clemency requests. [S7]

"This idea that life means life was a small comfort after all the trauma we had suffered to that point," Brindle said. [S7]

About the 2022 hearing: "To say I was retraumatized would be a gross understatement." [S7]

* * *

Kristian said she would come to clemency hearings for all four men if she had to. Four times a year, if it came to that. [S7]

"People shouldn't have to go through that," she said. "Life means life." [S7]

The committee changed the bill to let the parole board choose a wait of one to four years, then passed it on to the full Senate. [S7] The sources found for this story don't say what happened to it after that.

## Where it stands

Robert Power, Eric Coon and Robert Poppen pleaded guilty and were sentenced to life. [S2, S3, S5] Michael Lee Smith was convicted at trial and sentenced to life without parole on the conspiracy count. [S1] Walter Craft Jr. pleaded guilty to charges involving her murder. [S3] By January 2024, the governor had granted clemency to two people serving life without parole. Neither was from this case. [S7]

What's documented: Mary told Westport staff a key was missing before she was killed. [S2] Coon and Poppen used a key to get in. [S3]

Where the sources disagree: what Power offered, a pound of methamphetamine or two, and $10,000 for Smith or for each killer. [S1, S3, S4] How Smith's sentence reads. [S1, S5] How Amy Power's first name is spelled. [S2, S4] Whether Amy lost her keys in June or July. [S2, S8] How old Kristian was: 13 months in March 1994 (S8), or 14 months in July 1995. [S4, S7]

What's sworn but never decided in public: whether Mary asked for a new lock. Her mother said Mary told her she did. The landlord's employees said she didn't. [S2]

What no one has answered: which key Smith copied, the one Mary lent Power in February or one from Amy's lost key ring. The Supreme Court said it was unclear. [S2, S8]
