A golfer went looking for lost balls near Crofton, Nebraska [S2].

It should have been the kind of errand nobody remembers. A few minutes of searching near the Crofton Lakeview Golf Course, then back to the game.

Instead, the golfer found a young woman [S2].

Her name was Tammy Haas. She was 19, and she had been missing for six days [S1][S3]. Home was just across the river, in Yankton, South Dakota [S16].

Since then there has been a manslaughter charge, a jury trial, an acquittal, and a federal reward [S16][S1]. Her family has hired private investigators. A national news program has told her story [S3].

And more than thirty years later, the most basic question about her death still hasn't been answered in public.

## Homecoming Week

That September, Yankton High School was in the middle of homecoming, according to the Yankton Press & Dakotan [S4][S8].

Tammy Haas had turned 19 that spring. Her full name was Tamara Ann, but everyone called her Tammy. Her birthday was April 13 [S1].

Her mother was Nancy. She had a brother, Paul. Her boyfriend was Eric Stukel [S3].

The photograph the FBI would later share was taken in 1990. Tammy is smiling, her chin resting on her hand [S1].

It's an ordinary snapshot of a teenage girl. Years later, it became the picture on an FBI case page.

The public record keeps the rest of her life private. Where she worked, what she planned to do next, what made her laugh. Those things belong to the people who knew her, and they aren't ours to fill in.

What the record does keep is one night that month, and the six days after it.

## The Party Near Crofton

Tammy went to a party at a farm near Crofton on September 17, 1992 [S1][S3]. The Norfolk Daily News places the farm in Cedar County, Nebraska [S16]. Her boyfriend went with her [S1][S3].

That was the last day anyone reported seeing her, according to the Associated Press [S3].

Picture that farm at night. Then notice how little the record lets you see.

It doesn't say when Tammy left. It doesn't say where she went, or who she was with, or the last time anyone saw her alive.

The FBI's version is brief. She went to the party. She did not come home that night [S1].

By the next day, she had been reported missing [S16].

## Six Days

Then came the waiting.

It lasted until September 23, when the golfer went looking for those lost balls [S2][S3]. The search turned up Tammy, near the golf course and not far from where the party had been [S1][S3].

Six days of not knowing ended there. A different kind of not knowing began.

An autopsy gave investigators part of an answer. The FBI says it attributed her death to hyperflexion of her neck [S1]. The Press & Dakotan later reported that investigators pointed to either a severe blow to the neck or hyperflexion [S8].

That named the kind of injury. It didn't say how it happened, or where, or when.

That gap would outlast everything that came next.

## The Verdict

Three years passed.

Eric Stukel was charged with manslaughter in October 1995. According to the Norfolk Daily News, the charge came three days before the statute of limitations ran out [S16].

The case was filed in Cedar County [S16]. The Nebraska attorney general's office prosecuted it, the AP reported [S3]. The trial was held in Hartington, Nebraska, and Yankton attorney Mike Stevens defended Stukel [S4].

The jury acquitted him [S4][S16].

That verdict is the court's final word. Eric Stukel was found not guilty, and nothing in the public record since has changed that. What the jurors heard, and how they weighed it, isn't part of the record available to us.

The verdict closed the charge. It didn't close the case for either family.

## Two Families

A Yankton author published a novel based on the case in 2005 [S4].

Stevens told the Press & Dakotan he hadn't read it. He said the verdict hadn't ended things for the family. He also criticized people who wouldn't accept what twelve unbiased jurors had decided [S4].

Tammy's family wasn't finished. In 2015 they raised about $24,000 through social media and hired private investigators, according to the AP [S3]. That April, Dateline NBC featured Tammy in its Cold Case Spotlight [S2][S3].

The coverage that spring produced a small, telling mistake. An AP story attributed one line to Tammy's mother: "There's no way I doubt his innocence." [S3]

The AP later corrected it. Those words belonged to Dawn Stukel, Eric's mother [S3].

By then, two families had lived with the case for more than twenty years, from opposite sides of the same verdict.

## Where the Record Goes Quiet

This is where the story gets harder to pin down.

Even the place she was found is told two ways. The FBI says a ditch near where the party had been [S1]. Newspaper accounts describe a ravine near the Crofton Lakeview Golf Course [S3][S4][S8].

The trial year splits too. The 2005 Press & Dakotan and the Norfolk Daily News point to 1996 [S4][S16]. A 2021 Press & Dakotan story says 1995 [S8]. NBC spells the boyfriend's name Erick [S2].

Then there is the detail that's hardest to set down. According to NBC, authorities at the time believed Tammy had been in the trunk of a car before she was left where she was found [S2]. The reporting doesn't say which car, and no court ever established it.

One of Tammy's childhood friends, Sally Smith, told Dateline she believes there was a cover-up [S2]. No investigation has publicly backed that up.

Six years after Dateline, investigators came back to Yankton with a question of their own.

## Her Birthday

Tammy Haas would have turned 48 on April 13, 2021 [S1][S8].

That was the day the FBI and the Yankton Police Department held a news conference in Yankton [S7][S8]. They announced a reward of up to $15,000 [S1][S7].

Read the terms closely. The reward isn't for an arrest, and it isn't for a conviction. The FBI's poster asks for information that uncovers exactly how Tammy died [S1].

Michael Paul, the FBI special agent in charge, put it plainly: "What happened that night? Why did she die?" [S8]

Go back to that autopsy in 1992. It named the injury and stopped there.

Since then, a charge was filed, a trial was held, and a jury gave its verdict. Almost thirty years later, the federal government was still asking the question the autopsy left open.

The FBI files her case under two words: Unexplained Death [S1].

## What We Know Now

The FBI and Yankton Police said in 2021 that they were still investigating. They hoped the reward would bring people forward [S7].

Tammy's page on the FBI's website was still online as of October 10, 2026, reward and all [S1].

No one has said publicly when she left the party. No one has said where or when she was hurt, or how she came to be near that golf course.

Someone may know. If you are that person, the reward was made for you.
