On September 23, 1992, a golfer went looking for lost balls near the Crofton Lakeview Golf Course in Crofton, Nebraska.

In a ravine near the course, the golfer found something no one should ever find. It was the body of a young woman.

Her name was Tammy Haas. She was 19. She lived just across the state line in Yankton, South Dakota, and she had gone to a party in the Crofton area several days earlier.

She never made it home.

More than three decades later, there has been an investigation, a charge, a trial, and a verdict. There has been a federal reward. And there is still no official answer to the most basic question of all.

The FBI's public listing for Tammy doesn't say murder. It doesn't say accident.

It says two words: Unexplained Death.

* * *

Tamara Ann Haas was born on April 13, 1973. Everyone called her Tammy.

In the fall of 1992 she was 19 and living in Yankton. Her mother was Nancy Haas. Her brother was Paul. Her boyfriend at the time was Eric Stukel. (Some sources spell his first name Erick.)

The public record doesn't say much about Tammy's everyday life that fall. Where she worked, what she was planning, what made her laugh. Those memories belong to her family and the people who grew up with her, and they're theirs to share.

What the record does give us is a single September night, and the long silence that followed it.

* * *

On September 17, 1992, Tammy went to a party at a farm near Crofton with her boyfriend. That's the date the FBI lists for the case. The Associated Press reports it was the last day anyone saw her.

Right away, the timeline starts to blur.

Some local coverage puts the party on September 18 instead. Local reports also say Tammy was reported missing on the 18th. Who made that report, and at what hour, isn't something the public reporting makes clear.

What happened between the party and the moment someone noticed she was gone is the center of this whole mystery. The public record doesn't establish when Tammy left the farm. It doesn't establish where she went next. It doesn't name, with any certainty, the last person to see her alive.

The FBI's account is short. She went to the party. She did not return home that night.

Then came the waiting.

Days passed. Then, on September 23, the golfer walked into that ravine near the Crofton golf course.

* * *

An autopsy gave investigators one piece of the answer.

When the FBI and Yankton Police described the case publicly in 2021, they said Tammy's death was caused by "hyperflexation of her neck." NBC's Dateline described it more simply as a broken neck. A researcher who later studied the case has disputed that shorthand and described the injury differently. Without the medical records, no one outside the case can settle the difference.

What the autopsy finding doesn't say matters just as much. It describes the injury. It doesn't say who or what caused it, where it happened, or when.

There was one more detail, and it's the kind that stays with you.

According to NBC, authorities at the time believed Tammy had been in the trunk of a car before she was left in that ravine. That's the investigators' assessment as reported, not a finding proven in court. The reporting doesn't say which car.

If that assessment was right, Tammy didn't simply wander away from a party and fall. Someone moved her. And whoever did it has never told the story.

* * *

In 1995, three years after Tammy died, prosecutors in Cedar County, Nebraska, charged Eric Stukel with manslaughter. According to the Argus Leader, the charge was filed just days before a legal deadline would have run out.

The case went to trial in 1996 in Hartington, Nebraska. Stukel was represented by defense attorney Mike Stevens.

The jury found Stukel not guilty.

That verdict is the final word the courts have given on the charge. The details of what the jury heard, and why it decided the way it did, aren't something we could verify from the public record. We won't guess at them here.

For both families, the verdict didn't end anything.

In 2005, Stevens spoke with the Yankton Press & Dakotan about a novel based on the case, and about how accusations can follow a person long after a jury has acquitted him.

Ten years later, Tammy's family pushed the case back into the light. In 2015 they raised roughly $24,000 to hire private investigators, according to the Associated Press. That April, Dateline NBC featured Tammy in its Cold Case Spotlight.

One of Tammy's childhood friends, Sally Smith, told Dateline she believes there was a cover-up. That's her personal belief. No investigation has established it.

Even the news coverage got tangled. That spring, the AP had to run a correction. A line defending Stukel's innocence, "There's no way I doubt his innocence," had been attributed to Tammy's mother. It was actually said by Dawn Stukel, Eric Stukel's mother.

Two mothers. Two families. Both still living with that September, more than twenty years on.

* * *

Then, in 2021, the case took a turn that's quietly unsettling once you sit with it.

On April 13, 2021, FBI Minneapolis and the Yankton Police Department held a news conference in Yankton. It was the day Tammy would have turned 48.

They announced a reward of up to $15,000.

Not for an arrest. Not for a conviction. The FBI's poster asks for information that would help uncover *exactly how* Tammy Haas died.

Read that again. Nearly thirty years after a golfer found her, after a charge and a full jury trial, the official question still isn't "who." It's "how."

The FBI and Yankton Police said the investigation was continuing, and that they hoped the reward would bring people forward.

As of October 2026, the FBI's listing for Tammy is still up, and it still lists the reward.

* * *

So here is where things stand.

**What's documented:** Tammy Haas, 19, of Yankton, went to a farm party near Crofton, Nebraska, in September 1992. Her body was found in a ravine near the Crofton Lakeview Golf Course on September 23. An autopsy attributed her death to hyperflexation of her neck. Eric Stukel was charged with manslaughter in 1995 and acquitted by a jury in 1996. In 2021, the FBI and Yankton Police offered up to $15,000 for information about how she died.

**What's reported but not proven:** NBC reported that authorities believed she had been in a car trunk before she was left in the ravine. A childhood friend believes there was a cover-up.

**What no one has publicly answered:** Whether the party was on the 17th or the 18th. When Tammy left. Who she was with when she was hurt. Where the injury happened. How she ended up in that ravine, and who put her there.

Investigators have said it plainly: they believe someone out there has information that could finally explain how Tammy died. Whatever that is, it has been sitting with someone for more than thirty years.
