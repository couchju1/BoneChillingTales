# Ch 3: Point of View: The Chorus and Free Indirect Discourse

## Core Idea
The story is told by a chorus of dozens of witnesses (the afterword lists well over a hundred people interviewed): relatives, lovers, neighbors, cops, lawyers, cellmates, reporters, victims' widows. Each speaks through close third person that slides into their private thoughts. No viewpoint is authoritative. The killer does get occasional interior passages (drinking, brooding, jealousy), but at the decisive moments he's seen from outside or through others, so he stays an enigma.

## Frameworks
- **The Chorus**: the killer's viewpoint sections are far fewer than everyone else's combined. The center is surrounded by testimony.
  - Who belongs in it: family (protective, guilty), lovers (romantic, frightened), employers and co-workers (practical, baffled), accomplices and bystanders (sometimes disturbed or high, rendered fully), law enforcement (procedural), lawyers (strategic), victims' families (grieving, ordinary), media (opportunistic, self-aware).
- **Free Indirect Discourse (FID)**: third-person past tense carrying the character's own thought patterns, without "she thought" tags. Start with an external action → slide into an unmarked thought in their idiom → return to action.
- **Introduce Through the Meeter**: a new character's first portrait is filtered through whoever meets them first, with that person's suspicion, envy, or admiration. (The rights-buyer is first described through a distrustful rival.)
- **The Opaque Center**: at the crime itself, the killer is external: what he said and did, nothing of what he felt. His self-explanations come later, through transcripts and letters. Other characters (an interviewer, a psychiatrist, a lawyer) judge his accounts. The narrator never does.
- **Interpretation by Proxy**: whenever the book needs analysis (why his account of the murders sounds like every hustler's, what a judge's ruling means), it gives that analysis to a character, in that character's vocabulary.
- **Conflicting Testimony Left Standing**: two witnesses, two versions, back to back, unresolved.
- **Witness Relay** (climaxes only): at the execution, viewpoint rotates rapidly among a half-dozen witnesses, sometimes inside a single paragraph: the uncle, the lawyer, the producer, the priest, the prosecutor. Each registers a different fragment. This is the one place the "one viewpoint per section" rule bends on purpose.

## Viewpoint Selection Rules
- Choose the viewpoint with the most at stake, or the most unexpected angle (the stoned accomplice in the truck, the stranger who finds the body).
- Ordinary bystanders get full, serious viewpoint time.
- Switch viewpoints at section breaks, except in deliberate witness relays at the climax.
- A minor character can get one section and never come back.

## Anti-patterns
- **Omniscient explanation** ("What Gary didn't realize was…").
- **Psychologizing narrator**: diagnosis appears only in a report or testimony, and doctors can disagree.
- **Inhabiting the killer during the act**: it turns him into a protagonist or a monster.
- **Casual head-hopping** outside a deliberate climax relay.

## Key Takeaways
1. Tell it through many witnesses. The killer's interior is rationed and absent at the act.
2. Introduce each newcomer through the eyes of whoever meets them.
3. Give every piece of analysis to a character.
4. At the climax, relay among witnesses for a mosaic of fragments.
