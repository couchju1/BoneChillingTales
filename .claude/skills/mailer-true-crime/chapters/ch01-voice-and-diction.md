# Ch 1: Voice & Diction: The Flat Western Sentence

## Core Idea
The narrator is not "Norman Mailer." The famous baroque, opinionated, first-person Mailer is gone completely. In his place is a plain, nearly invisible voice that takes on the vocabulary, grammar, and moral vision of whoever the passage belongs to. The book opens on a six-year-old girl falling out of an apple tree, told in four short sentences. That's the whole method in miniature: simple facts, a child's scale, no comment.

## Frameworks
- **The Borrowed Voice**: each passage is narrated in the idiom of the person at its center. A cousin's sections are chatty, practical, small-town colloquial ("swell," "real polite," "damn good"). The girlfriend's drift: sensual, stoned, fatalistic, full of "you know" and "kind of." A lawyer's are careful and self-justifying. A media producer's are quick, strategic, and self-conscious.
  - How: before drafting, list 10–15 words and phrases the subject actually uses (from interviews, letters, transcripts). Write the passage in that register. Never use a word the person wouldn't.
- **The Absent Author**: no thesis statements, no lessons, no "little did she know." Meaning builds up from juxtaposition and accumulated detail. Even judgment words are nearly always a character's.
- **Declarative Drive**: short subject-verb-object sentences in simple past. Heavy use of plain verbs ("got," "went," "said," "told"). Fragments when the mind works in fragments: dropped subjects ("Didn't want to talk about it." "Felt pretty rattled.").

## Diction Rules
- Plain Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones. Regional and class idiom kept intact.
- Prison slang, drug slang, and 1970s youth slang are rendered exactly ("vibes," "bummer," "freaked," "on the bricks").
- Profanity and ugly language, slurs included, appear exactly as the person would use them, inside their thoughts or speech. The narrator never endorses them or cleans them up.
- Exact numbers and brand names: wages per hour, trailer dimensions, gallons of gas, car models, a paper-towel rack's brand.
- Similes are allowed **only when they belong to the viewpoint character**: a homespun comparison for Western voices, a cultivated or cinematic one for Eastern voices. No similes come from the author.

## Rhythm
- A paragraph is a single floe of text, median ~37 words, set off by white space (see Ch 2).
- Runs of short sentences, then one long "and… and…" run-on for a rush of feeling or memory.
- Free indirect thought slides in untagged: "She didn't know. Maybe he'd change."
- Understatement at peak moments. The bigger the event, the flatter the sentence.
- A dry, near-invisible irony sometimes comes from the borrowed idiom itself, as when a scene ends by echoing the character's own phrase about how clean a place was.

## Anti-patterns
- **Purple horror prose** ("a blood-soaked night of unspeakable terror").
- **Author's metaphors**: if a simile appears, it must sound like the viewpoint character's.
- **Journalistic summary** in place of scenes.
- **Uniform voice**: if every section sounds the same, the technique has failed.
- **Mockery of the subjects' speech**: dialect is rendered with respect, never as a joke.

## Worked Example (original pastiche: fictional case)
> Darla got the call at work. She was on register two and it was the lunch rush, so she told her sister she'd call back. Her sister said, "Darla, it's about Wade." Darla said she figured.
>
> Wade had been out eleven days. He'd been real good for about nine of them.
>
> She thought about it while she rang up a man buying motor oil and a bag of ice. You didn't sponsor a cousin out of Point of the Mountain without thinking about it. Her husband had said, "It's your funeral." That wasn't a nice thing to say, but Ron wasn't always nice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Delete yourself. Every sentence belongs to a witness.
2. Aim for a median sentence of about 10 words; half your sentences at 10 words or fewer.
3. Change the diction with each new point of view, and let it get more sophisticated as outsiders arrive.
4. Similes and judgment words belong to characters only.
5. The flatter the telling, the more terrible the event feels.
