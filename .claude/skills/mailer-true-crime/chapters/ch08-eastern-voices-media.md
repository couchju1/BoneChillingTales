# Ch 8: Eastern Voices: The Media, the Story-Buyer, the End

## Core Idea
Book Two opens when the condemned man refuses appeals and demands execution. The world arrives: a self-dramatizing first lawyer-agent, rival bidders for his life rights, a producer who wins them, national reporters and talk-show bookers, civil-liberties lawyers, the state attorney's office, the interviewer who prepares hundreds of questions. Book Two is slightly *longer* than Book One. The voice stays unauthored, but the register rises: more educated diction, longer sentences, cinematic and literary similes, deal language. The book watches its own story get made.

## Book Two's Movement (real Part titles)
1. **In the Reign of Good King Boaz**: a grandiose young lawyer-writer takes charge of the condemned man's affairs. Press conferences; the circus begins. The lovers' suicide pact with smuggled sleeping pills, and the aftermath.
2. **Exclusive Rights**: bidding, negotiation, and the contract for life rights. The producer outmaneuvers rivals.
3. **The Hunger Strike**: the pardons board, the state's lawyers, third-party "next friend" suits, the prisoner's hunger strike to be allowed to see his girlfriend.
4. **The Holiday Season**: Christmas on death row. Prepared interview questions smuggled in and answered on tape.
5. **Pressures**: deadlines, the family's lawyers, the media pressing, the last weekend.
6. **Into the Light**: the final night (a party in the prison), the last tape, dawn, the legal race up to the morning, the firing squad.
7. **The Fading of the Heart**: television, the remains, scattering, the news ebbing, the kiss-and-tell, one short section per survivor across the seasons.

## Frameworks
- **The Register Shift**: when the viewpoint passes to outsiders, sentences lengthen (median 11 words, 8–12% over 30 words versus 5% in Book One) and similes go cosmopolitan (cinema, magicians, wildlife). Deal terms, deadlines, and exclusives fill the prose.
- **The Story-Buyer as Viewpoint**: the producer is a central consciousness. He's ambitious, touchy about his reputation as a buyer of notorious lives, keeps calculating, and is increasingly moved. He first appears through a distrustful rival's eyes. For your own work, put the media, and even the making of your own account, inside the story.
- **The Interviewer's Judgment**: the journalist who shapes the interview questions judges the subject's answers on the page. When the killer talks about the murders, his account goes generic, and it's the interviewer, not the author, who says so.
- **Interview Tapes**: long script-format excerpts (see Ch 4) where his philosophy (karma, reincarnation, courage, convict honor) and his evasions come out in his own words.
- **The Countdown**: the final Parts run on a clock (stays granted and lifted, last visitors, an all-night prison party, dawn, the ride to the execution room), then Witness Relay at the firing squad (Ch 3, Ch 6).
- **After the End**: autopsy, cremation and scattering, the media afterlife, and a coda of fates that ends on the most isolated survivor, his mother, alone, facing threats and songwriters' letters with defiance.

## Rules
- Media people get the same empathy as everyone else: shown in their competence and their compromises.
- Reproduce headlines, wire items, and contract terms as documents.
- The execution is told with maximum procedural restraint: the last words, the sounds, one witness's exact image of the blood.

## Anti-patterns
- **Satirizing the press from on high**: let their deals and self-justifications show the circus.
- **A climactic moral summation after the execution.**

## Key Takeaways
1. When the case goes public, change the chorus and raise the register.
2. Put the story-buyer and the interviewer, and their judgments, inside the book.
3. The countdown is minute by minute, and the execution is a witness relay.
4. End in a coda of survivors' fates, closing on the loneliest one.
