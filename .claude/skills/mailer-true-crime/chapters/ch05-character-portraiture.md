# Ch 5: Character Portraiture: Ordinary Lives, Total Attention

## Core Idea
Every person, from the killer's cousin to a motel manager on his last ordinary day, gets portrayed with the same patient, non-judgmental attention. Character comes from concrete detail: jobs, money, cars, marriages, religion, food, sex, debts. It doesn't come from the author's adjectives. The overall effect is a cross-section of a whole community, and much of the book's moral force comes from that.

## Frameworks
- **The Biography Burst**: When a new person enters, give a compact passage of their life history in their own idiom: where they grew up, marriages, kids, work, church standing, one telling anecdote. Then go straight back to the present scene.
- **Material Specificity**: Characters are defined by what they own, earn, eat, and drive: the wage at the insulation plant, the trailer, the used truck, the beer, the shoe-repair shop, the payments. Money worries are constant and exact.
- **The Damaged Romantic** (the girlfriend archetype): a young woman with a string of early marriages and children, sensual, impulsive, partly mystical, who drifts. She's rendered with total empathy and no condescension. Her logic is given its full weight.
- **The Contradictory Killer**: charming, artistic, well-read in a self-taught way, generous, funny, and also violent, jealous, self-pitying, manipulative. Show every side through different witnesses. Never reduce him to one.
- **The Ordinary Victim**: decent, hardworking, newly married, religious, saving for something. Their goodness is shown through small domestic details (how they fought, how they made up), never stated.
- **The Family Chorus**: the mother (loyal, ailing, isolated), relatives who took the risk of sponsoring him, a brother living with the shame. Each carries a share of the history.

## Rules
- No villain or hero labels from the narrator. Judgment belongs to the characters and the reader.
- Treat religious belief, especially Mormonism in a Utah setting, as lived texture, not satire.
- **Belief rendered straight**: characters' supernatural convictions (a haunted house, an apparition "attaching" itself to someone, reincarnation, karma, astrology, synchronicity) are told in their own terms, never debunked or winked at. The mother's ghost story can close the book.
- **Disturbed minds rendered fully**: an accomplice's psychosis or drug-memory runs in long, associative FID paragraphs, sometimes with ugly language. Rendered with neither mockery nor commentary.
- Sex is reported candidly and plainly, in the participants' words: neither prurient nor coy.
- Humor comes from the characters (wisecracks, mishaps), never from the narrator mocking them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stereotype shorthand** ("white trash," "monster," "angel").
- **Back-story dumps for minor characters** that run longer than their role justifies. Keep bursts compact.
- **Sanctifying victims** with eulogy language. Show them in specifics, flaws included.

## Worked Example (biography burst, original)
> Kenny Ostrander was twenty-six and had been married to Jolene for two years in April. He'd met her at a stake dance in Spanish Fork. She was the one who asked him. He liked to tell that. He'd bought the tire shop off his uncle with eleven thousand dollars he'd saved hauling hay, and they lived in the back half of a duplex on Third North with a crib already set up, though the baby wasn't due till fall.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every character gets the same serious attention.
2. Character = material facts + idiom, not adjectives.
3. Show the killer's contradictions through many witnesses.
4. Make victims fully, specifically alive before the crime.
