# Ch 4: Dialogue, Letters, Transcripts: The Documentary Layer

## Core Idea
Much of the book's power comes from primary material set into the flat narration: the killer's long, mystical prison love letters; the police interrogation and the condemned man's recorded interviews, laid out like a script; newspaper items with datelines; legal filings; letters from the public and family. Flat narration makes the documents ring louder, and the documents vouch for the narration.

## Frameworks
- **Dialogue as Characterization**: short, colloquial, often banal, frequently evasive. People talk past each other. Big truths come out as offhand remarks.
  - Tags (measured): "said" ≈ 68% of all speech tags, "told" ≈ 16%, "asked" ≈ 11%. Everything else is rare. Little stage direction.
  - **Heavy use of reported speech**: the high "told" count reflects how often talk is summarized without quotes ("She told him the rent was due Friday." "Dale said, Sure."). Mix direct quotes for the lines that matter with indirect report for the rest.
  - An unquoted comma-led reply ("Dale said, Sure.") gives a dry, oral-history feel.
- **The Script Transcript**: interrogations and recorded interviews are laid out like a screenplay, never with "Q:/A:" labels:
  ```
  March 4, 1998 2:15 a.m.
  WADE
      Why am I here?
  OSTRANDER
      You tell me, Wade.
  ```
  A date and time header, the speaker's name in capitals on its own line, the speech indented below. Narrative sections before and after show the participants' strategy and reactions (the prosecutor coaching the interrogator; the interviewer judging the answers).
- **The Letter Section**: letters are set off as indented text. The afterword says Mailer **chose the killer's best letters and kept them nearly uncut,** deliberately showing him at better than his usual level so his mind's effect on the girlfriend comes through. Letters from others (a victim's relative, the girlfriend's mother) keep their own errors and run-ons.
- **The News Item**: masthead, dateline, wire-style lead ("SPANISH FORK, Utah, Aug. 4—A man charged in…", a fictional example). It shows the case turning into public property. Small trims are allowed.
- **The Lettered Anecdote**: a character's stories can be framed through the listener's ear, even as an A-B-C list, to show how that person tells things.

## Rules
- Mark documents clearly (indent, date, sender/recipient, source).
- Excerpt; don't dump. Pick the passage that shows character or a turning point.
- For the subject's own spoken words, trim for decency and clarity, never to improve them, and disclose that in the afterword.
- Juxtapose a tender letter against the writer's violence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Polished, witty dialogue.**
- **Paraphrasing a great document** instead of quoting it.
- **Editorial framing** ("In this chilling letter…").
- **Generic Q/A transcripts**: use named speakers.

## Worked Example (original, fictional)
```
August 3, 1998 11:40 p.m.
OSTRANDER
    Where were you around ten?
WADE
    Driving. I drive around. It's a free country, last I heard.
OSTRANDER
    Driving where, Wade?
WADE
    Around.
```
> Detective Ostrander had been told by the County Attorney to keep it friendly, so he kept it friendly.

## Key Takeaways
1. Transcripts use a time header, a capitalized speaker, and indented speech.
2. Use "said" most, "told" for reported speech, almost nothing else.
3. Show the subject's letters at their best, and say so.
4. Datelined news items mark the shift to the public story.
