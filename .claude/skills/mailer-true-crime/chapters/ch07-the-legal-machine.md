# Ch 7: The Legal Machine: Arrest, Trial, Sentence, Appeals

## Core Idea
The justice system is rendered as a human machine run by ordinary professionals: public defenders, a prosecutor, a judge, psychiatrists, jailers, the warden, the pardons board. Each has a career, vanities, doubts, and a working-day point of view. Procedure is explained by the people doing it, in their own terms. Mailer never lectures on the death penalty. The machinery's absurdities and gravity come through from inside.

## Frameworks
- **Professional Interiority**: lawyers and officials get FID sections with their career thinking: why they took the case, how they read the jury, their pride in a small-town practice, their tactical regrets. Their judgments are self-serving and human.
- **Procedure as Suspense**: competency evaluations, the trial, the mitigation/penalty hearing, appeals, stays, and pardons board hearings are told step by step. The reader learns the rules as the characters argue over them.
- **The Defendant Who Wants to Die**: the central irony. The condemned man demands his own execution and fires lawyers who try to save him. The system, activists, and family fight over his right to die. Present every side's logic straight.
- **Strategy Around the Transcript**: before an interrogation or hearing transcript, a section shows a lawyer's tactics (the prosecutor coaching the interrogator to be friendly and read the rights, while avoiding talking to the suspect himself so he won't become a witness). Then the script-format transcript (Ch 4).
- **Institutional Life**: jail and prison routine (the cell, the guards, the cellmates, medication, hunger strikes, contraband, visiting rules) told concretely through inmates and staff.

## Proportion (from the original)
The trial Part (sanity hearing, cellmate interlude, insanity question, prosecution, defense, sentence) is only ~7% of the book. Real trials are short. The long fight comes after the sentence (Book Two, ~half the book): pardons board, state supreme court, federal stays, "next friend" suits, and the state attorney's office, each with a named human operator.

## Rules
- Explain legal mechanics only through a character's thinking or dialogue, never as narrator exposition.
- Give both prosecution and defense a sympathetic viewpoint.
- Keep dates and procedural milestones exact. They're the spine of this part of the book.
- Psychiatric opinions appear as quoted reports or testimony, and they can conflict.

## Anti-patterns
- **Op-ed about capital punishment.** The book shows the arguments. It doesn't make them.
- **Courtroom-drama clichés** (surprise witnesses, thunderous speeches). Real trials are short, procedural, anticlimactic.
- **Faceless officials**: every functionary gets a name and a point of view.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make the system human: named professionals with careers and doubts.
2. Procedure is suspense. Lay it out step by step, accurately.
3. Present the death-penalty conflict through its participants, not the author.
4. Verbatim transcript at the decisive moments.
